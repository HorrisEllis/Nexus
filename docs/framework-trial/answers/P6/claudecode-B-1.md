**Don't remove them yet. Have one direct, private conversation first. Ask what's going on, then agree a changed role or a clean exit. Only after that, if nothing has moved, take them off the critical path.**

**What a good outcome looks like**
- At minimum, the project stops depending on work that isn't arriving.
- Better, the contributor ends up with a commitment they can actually keep, whether that's smaller, later or different, and the team stays intact.
- Best, the lead learns why this happened, and the project changes how it assigns work so it doesn't happen again.

**What holds regardless**
- It's a volunteer project, so nobody can be made to work. The only things that work are motivation, fit and clarity.
- Three missed deadlines in a row is a pattern, not bad luck.
- Their past work was good, so skill isn't the problem. Something changed: their time, their life, their interest, or the work itself.
- With only four people, one person is 25% of capacity, and how the lead handles this will be watched by the other two.

**What's missing**
So far the lead has only sent reminders. Reminders tell someone a deadline exists, and they already know that. Nobody has asked *why*. The contributor has also gone quiet about it: no warning, no request for more time. That silence tells you something. It often means they're overloaded or embarrassed, not that they don't care. It's also unclear whether these deadlines were ever realistic, and whether anything depends on this person beyond their own tasks.

**Two options**
1. **Remove them now.** It's quick and clear, and the team sees the lead protecting the project. But it treats a cause nobody has diagnosed. It loses someone with proven skill, may look harsh to the other two, and leaves the lead short-handed with the same gap.
2. **Talk, then restructure.** In a private conversation, without blame: "You've missed the last three. Your earlier work was strong, so I'm assuming something changed. What's going on, and what can you realistically commit to?" Then pick one:
   - smaller tasks that nothing else waits on;
   - a pause, with the door left open;
   - a graceful step back that you both agree on.

   In the meantime, move their critical tasks to someone else so the project isn't held hostage.

**Recommendation: option 2.** It costs one conversation, keeps option 1 available, and protects the project right away by moving critical work off them now.

**Where it could fail, and how to guard against it**
- *Weakest assumption:* that they'll be honest and keep the reset commitment. To guard against it, set one short, concrete next deliverable with a date. If that's missed too, or they don't reply within about a week, the lead steps them back, kindly and plainly, with no fourth round.
- *Risk: the other two see favoritism.* Tell them the work has been reassigned and a check-in has happened. Keep the reasons private.
- *Risk: the lead caused this,* through unclear scope or unrealistic deadlines. Ask about that directly in the conversation, and fix it for everyone if so.

**What this rests on, and what would prove it wrong**
- The advice depends on the claim that the cause can be fixed or at least understood. If the conversation shows they've lost interest, or they don't engage at all, then removal (framed as stepping back, with thanks) is the right call. Waiting longer would then be unfair to the other three.
- If someone else's work is blocked right now, move that work today, whatever else happens.
- If they keep the smaller commitment, the bet paid off: you keep a good contributor at lower risk.
