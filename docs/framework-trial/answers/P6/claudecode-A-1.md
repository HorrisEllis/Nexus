**Don't remove them yet. Have one direct, private conversation first, then change their role to fit what they can actually deliver. Removal stays the fallback, with a clear trigger.**

**What the facts tell us**

- Three missed deadlines that they agreed to, with no change after reminders, is a real pattern. It is not bad luck. The lead is right to act.
- Their past work was good. That points away from lack of skill or lack of care. In volunteer projects, a good contributor who suddenly stops delivering usually has something going on: a new job, family or health trouble, burnout, or quiet disagreement with the project's direction. They may also feel embarrassed and be avoiding the subject.
- Reminders don't work because they only repeat the deadline. They never ask why the deadline was missed. The missing step is a real conversation, not more pressure.
- Volunteers can't be compelled. The real lever is matching commitments to how much time they actually have.
- On a team of four, one stalled person holds up a quarter of the work. The other three also notice how the lead handles it. If nothing happens, resentment grows. If the person is dropped abruptly, everyone learns the project is cold.

**What the lead should do**

1. **Talk privately, by call or face to face.** Start with the facts and no judgment: "You've missed the last three deadlines. Your earlier work was really good, so I'm guessing something has changed. What's going on?" Then listen.
2. **Offer honest options.** Make it easy to say "I can't do this right now":
   - smaller, clearly defined tasks;
   - a lighter role, such as reviewing or advising;
   - a set break (for example six weeks) that ends with a check-in;
   - stepping back with thanks, and a standing welcome to return.
3. **Agree on one small, concrete next commitment** with a date one to two weeks away. Pick something they choose and believe they can finish. Write it down. Also agree that if they start slipping, they will tell the lead before the deadline, not after it.
4. **Take their tasks off the critical path now.** Reassign or split anything that blocks the others, so the project stops waiting while this gets sorted out.
5. **Be clear about what happens next.** "If this next one slips without word from you, I'll move your tasks to others and we'll treat you as stepping back." This is fair warning, not a threat.
6. **Follow through either way.** If they deliver, thank them, keep tasks small and rebuild gradually. If they don't, or they don't reply, take them off active work kindly and in private. Thank them for what they contributed and leave the door open. Tell the team in a neutral sentence and give no personal details.

**Why this beats removing them now**

It costs one conversation and one or two weeks. In return it may keep a proven, capable contributor, which is hard to replace on a team this size. It treats a volunteer as a person whose situation changed, not as an underperforming employee. It also shows the rest of the team that the lead is fair. If removal does come later, it will be clearly justified.

**Risks and uncertainties**

- They may agree to everything and then miss again. Steps 4 and 5 limit the damage and put a time limit on the wait.
- The cause may be private, such as health. Don't press for details. All the lead needs to know is what they can realistically commit to.
- If the project faces a hard external deadline, reassign their work immediately and have the conversation alongside that, not before it.
- If the cause turns out to be conflict with the lead or the project's direction, the problem is bigger than one person's deadlines and needs addressing on its own.
